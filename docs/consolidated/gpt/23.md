## 1. What exactly changes?

The line-local curvature condition is:

[

=========

\sum_\rho
\left(\frac12+it-\rho\right)^{-2}.

By harmonicity of (\log|\xi|),

[

==========================

# \partial_\sigma^2\log|\xi(\sigma+it)|\big|_{\sigma=1/2}

-\partial_t^2\log|\xi(1/2+it)|.
]

So

[
\Phi_2(t)<0

means:

[
\boxed{
\text{the critical line is a local crest of }\log|\xi|.
}
]

Under RH, this crest condition is expected/necessary: line zeros produce the right concavity. The formulas graph already records this as the PH3 node, “RH (\Rightarrow \Phi_2<0),” not as a full equivalence.

Your new finding says the converse fails:

[

\Phi_2(t)<0\ \forall t

Why? Because an off-line quartet contributes a positive curvature bump, but that bump can be drowned by the negative on-line background at high height.

## 2. Why (\Phi_2) misses far-off-line high zeros

For an off-line mirror pair with half-width

[
a=|\beta-\tfrac12|,
]

the curvature contribution is

[
C_a(\tau)
=========

\frac{2(a^2-\tau^2)}{(a^2+\tau^2)^2}.
]

It has positive peak

[
C_a(0)=\frac{2}{a^2}.
]

Near-line zeros, where (a\to0), produce a huge spike:

[
C_a(0)\to\infty.
]

So (\Phi_2) detects near-line off-line zeros very well.

But for far-off-line zeros, (a\le 1/2), the peak is bounded below/around a finite scale:

[
C_a(0)\ge 8,
]

while the on-line background grows roughly like

[
|\Phi_2(t)|\sim \frac{(\log t)^2}{\pi^2}.
]

So at sufficiently high height, the negative background can dominate the positive bump, and

[
\Phi_2(t)
]

can remain negative even though an off-line zero exists.

That is the blind spot:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{(\Phi_2) detects near-line off-line zeros, but can miss far-off-line high zeros.}
}
]

Your verification with injected far-off-line quartets at high (\gamma) is exactly the expected failure mode.
